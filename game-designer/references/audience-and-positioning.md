## Establish the project's purpose

Record whether the goal is commercial income, artistic expression, learning, a community experience, or a combination. Preserve the user's priorities. A personal gift or experimental art game does not need a market-sized audience to justify its existence. For commercial work, distinguish a sustainable release from an exceptional breakout; do not imply that either can be guaranteed.

In [design-brief.md](../assets/templates/design-brief.md), describe the intended players through relevant behavior: games they enjoy, desired experience, platform, available session length, and tolerated complexity. Avoid inventing demographic facts. Capture team capacity, budget constraints already supplied, and the smallest release that can express the promise. Treat audience and scope as design inputs.

## Build a useful comparison set

Choose a manageable set of directly relevant games, including recognizable successes, modest releases, and apparent misses. Compare player activity, presentation, complexity, scope, and release context. Do not choose only famous hits or compare a tiny first project to a mature game with years of updates.

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Player promise | The experience communicated and actually offered |
| Core activity | The repeated actions and decisions |
| Expected features | What genre players appear to consider necessary |
| Distinction | The premise, interaction, style, or structure that differentiates it |
| Evidence | Official pages, observed play, attributed reviews, dated disclosures |
| Uncertainty | Missing sales, unknown promotion, different platforms or audiences |

Play representative games when access permits. Otherwise distinguish store-page analysis, reviews, footage, and direct experience. Read positive and negative reactions for specific unmet needs, including whether the complaint is relevant to this project's audience. Do not infer revenue, profitability, or causation from review count, silence, or appearance alone. A game with little visible attention is an apparent comparison case, not proven evidence of bad design.

## Write a recognizable promise

Write a short sentence describing what players do and what makes it appealing. Anchor it in a familiar activity when that improves understanding, then state the distinctive element. Prefer something the game can demonstrate over “unique,” “immersive,” or an inventory of features.

For example: “Raise tiny rays whose personalities change how they explore your aquarium.” Treat this as an original candidate pitch, not a proven market opportunity. Identify what must exist in the build for the promise to be honest: observable personalities, meaningful differences during exploration, and care interactions that support attachment.

Distinction can come from theme, setting, interaction, social context, or execution. Do not force unrelated genres together to manufacture novelty. Evaluate any combination for comprehension, development cost, and whether its parts strengthen the same experience. Keep a short list of explicit exclusions to protect scope.

## Test presentation and play separately

Prepare truthful screenshots, a brief gameplay clip, and a concise description using the current build. Label concept art, mockups, or simulated footage clearly. Do not present planned features as implemented or use a trailer to imply interactions the game lacks. Choose material that communicates the actual activity and visual identity.

For a presentation test, show the material without explanation and ask: “What would you expect to do?”, “Who might enjoy this?”, and “What, if anything, makes you want to try it?” Record the participant's genre familiarity and what they actually saw. A comprehensible premise and curiosity are useful findings; neither establishes enjoyable play.

For a gameplay test, observe whether the experience fulfills the promise, where interest changes, and what participants choose to continue doing. Follow [playtesting-and-evidence.md](playtesting-and-evidence.md). Compare stated expectations with observed experience. A compelling pitch with weak play suggests a design problem; satisfying play with misunderstood presentation suggests a communication problem. Keep both explanations open until evidence supports one.

Prepare publishable materials and recruitment drafts as needed. Public posting, outreach, and account actions require applicable user authorization already present; do not contact people or spend money merely because testing would be useful.

## Interpret commercial evidence carefully

Define every reported number: source, date, platform, population, observation window, and whether it is measured, estimated, or self-reported. Keep impressions, visits, wishlists, demo use, purchases, revenue, and profit distinct. Avoid attributing a change to a trailer, feature, or campaign when several factors changed together.

Do not invent universal wishlist targets, conversion rates, demo-length thresholds, revenue forecasts, or hit probabilities. Use project-specific comparisons and explain uncertainty. A few enthusiastic responses do not estimate demand across a market. Engagement from developers or existing fans may differ from behavior by prospective customers. Paid and organic traffic can represent different contexts.

Before advising on Steam visibility, demo rules, notifications, festivals, fees, or release requirements, check the relevant current Steamworks documentation and cite it with the access date. Distinguish official rules from practitioner interpretation. Treat historical books and postmortems as context, not current platform specifications. Apply the same discipline to other storefronts.

## Choose the next bounded action

Summarize the strongest evidence, the most consequential uncertainty, and the cheapest useful next experiment. Examples include revising the pitch, prototyping the promised interaction, improving screenshots, testing with genre players, or cutting a feature that obscures the core experience. Record it in [experiment-card.md](../assets/templates/experiment-card.md).

Advance when the available evidence supports the next investment, while preserving uncertainty. Revisit positioning when the game changes or the evidence contradicts it. If commercial validation is unavailable, continue preparing a reviewable prototype and presentation; mark demand as unknown instead of claiming market fit or blocking all design work.
