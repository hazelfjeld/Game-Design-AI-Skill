# Design foundations

Use this procedure to shape ideas, diagnose games, and review features. These procedures, questions, and examples are original synthesis. For the work of Tracy Fullerton, Jesse Schell, and Raph Koster, see [Sources](sources.md).

## 1. Define the intended experience and audience

Write one experience statement: “For this audience, doing this activity should produce this experience.” Translate adjectives into observable behavior without treating that behavior as proof of an internal feeling.

Example: “Players who enjoy caring for creatures should become attached to individual rays through recognizable habits and responsive interactions.” Possible evidence includes recognizing a particular creature, describing a favorite interaction, or voluntarily revisiting it. A longer session alone does not establish attachment.

Record audience familiarity, attention, play setting, and access needs. Distinguish facts from assumptions. Reuse supplied information; ask only when an answer would materially change the design. Respect the available team, tools, and time.

Choose two or three experience priorities. Resolve conflicts explicitly: demanding precision and relaxed exploration can coexist through optional challenges, but imposing both on every interaction may undermine the intended audience's experience.

## 2. Describe the loop through player behavior

Describe one complete cycle in concrete terms:

**Situation → information noticed → action or choice → feedback → changed situation.**

For each transition, identify what the player understands, influences, and values. Replace “explore, collect, upgrade” with what makes one exploration decision different from another.

Separate three scales when useful:

- **Moment:** steer a ray toward a current and feel its response.
- **Session:** choose an expedition, discover an interaction, and bring something home.
- **Longer progression:** develop a habitat or repertoire that changes future possibilities.

Check whether progression adds behavior or only increases numbers. Repetition may support comfort, expression, or sensory pleasure; do not force strategic tradeoffs everywhere. Where strategy is intended, ensure different actions become sensible under different conditions.

## 3. Choose mechanisms that support the experience

Select mechanisms deliberately instead of assuming every game needs combat, scarcity, levels, or randomized rewards.

| Intended experience | Design move to consider | Failure to watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Mastery | Make improvement perceptible; vary a learned problem | Difficulty increases without useful feedback |
| Suspense | Reveal enough information to support anticipation | Outcomes feel arbitrary or unavoidable |
| Curiosity | Establish a pattern, then invite a testable question | Secrets require exhaustive blind searching |
| Attachment | Give individuals recognizable behavior and responsive interactions | Care becomes compulsory maintenance |
| Comfort | Offer dependable rhythms, control over pace, and recoverable mistakes | Repetition supplies no satisfying response |
| Expression | Let choices produce visible, meaningful differences | One numerical optimum erases personal preference |

Treat learning and pattern recognition as useful explanations for some engagement, not a universal account of every player's enjoyment. Describe the proposed causal connection: “This behavior may support this experience because…” Then identify what observation could challenge it.

## 4. Inspect the design from several perspectives

Use these original diagnostic questions selectively; do not turn them into a mandatory scorecard.

- **Understanding:** Can a first-time player explain what happened and what they could try next?
- **Influence:** Which part of the outcome responds to the player's action, timing, interpretation, or expression?
- **Development:** Does a familiar action acquire new meaning as circumstances change?
- **Consequence:** Is the cost of experimenting proportionate to what the player can reasonably know?
- **Coherence:** Do rules, presentation, fiction, and rewards support the same experience priorities?
- **Interest:** What might the intended player voluntarily want to do again, and what evidence supports that hypothesis?

Distinguish a communication problem from a system problem. A clearer description can reveal an interesting decision; it cannot create one. More particle effects can strengthen readable feedback; they cannot establish why an outcome matters.

## 5. Build levels, problems, and teaching together

For each encounter or level, specify its purpose, required knowledge, available actions, and recognizable outcome. Introduce an action in a forgiving context, invite a small variation, then combine it with something already understood. Let players demonstrate understanding through play before adding another demand.

Vary constraints with purpose: space, timing, information, resources, or relationships. Avoid changing all of them at once when trying to diagnose difficulty. Offer optional depth for experienced players and useful recovery for learners.

Design hints to preserve agency: first direct attention, then explain a relevant relationship, then offer a solution if appropriate. Test onboarding with someone who has not watched development. Record interventions; a tutorial that works only because the designer explains it has not yet succeeded independently.

## 6. Make participation possible for different players

Check input, perception, comprehension, pacing, and recovery at the prototype stage. Consider remapping, alternatives to sustained holds or rapid presses, readable text, redundant cues, adjustable motion effects, pause behavior, and resumable sessions where relevant. Never rely on color alone for essential distinctions.

Separate challenge from unnecessary access barriers. Preserve the intended decision where possible while allowing different ways to perceive information or execute an action. Ask affected players about practical barriers rather than claiming an option makes the whole game accessible.

Account for different motivations within the audience. A collector, explorer, optimizer, and social player may value different outcomes. Support these differences intentionally; avoid assuming that completion speed or competitive performance measures everyone's success.

## 7. Diagnose the cause and test a small remedy

| Observed problem | Plausible cause | Small experiment |
|---|---|---|
| Players repeatedly miss the goal | Goal or feedback is unclear | Improve one cue; observe a fresh player |
| Players always choose one option | Its costs or benefits dominate | Change one relationship; inspect choices |
| Players stop experimenting | Failure is expensive or outcomes opaque | Reduce recovery cost or clarify consequences |
| Play feels busy but flat | Actions change little that players value | Remove chores; strengthen one consequential interaction |
| Players cannot plan | Too many simultaneous variables | Introduce one system later; compare comprehension |
| Familiar play becomes stale | Situations stop changing | Add a contextual variation before adding content volume |

Record the observation, competing explanations, proposed change, and expected evidence. Change the smallest useful part. Evaluate its benefits and costs. Keep, revise, or discard it; do not equate additional features with progress.
