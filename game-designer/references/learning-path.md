# Optional learning path

Use this path for requested learning or coaching. Do not require an experienced user to complete it before receiving design help or implementation. These exercises and milestones are original synthesis, informed by the perspectives attributed in [Sources](sources.md).

## Contents

1. Choose an appropriate entry point
2. Observe a game deliberately
3. Change one rule
4. Build the smallest useful prototype
5. Observe another player
6. Revise against evidence
7. Finish a small experience
8. Develop and assess a larger candidate
9. Coach toward independence

## 1. Choose an appropriate entry point

Infer skills from the conversation and existing work. A programmer may need practice testing experience goals; a designer may need implementation support. Do not confuse unfamiliar terminology with lack of ability.

Offer one exercise at a time, preferably using the learner's project. Adapt suggested timeboxes; they constrain scope without promising completion speed.

Choose an entry point based on evidence:

| Current difficulty | Start here |
|---|---|
| “I know what I like, but cannot explain why” | Observe a game |
| “My idea is a list of features” | Change one rule |
| “I cannot tell whether the idea works” | Build a prototype |
| “Everyone says it is good, but I am unsure” | Observe another player |
| “I keep expanding and never finish” | Finish a small experience |

Explain the output and learning goal. Let demonstrated capability satisfy a milestone without repeating exercises.

## 2. Observe a game deliberately

**Exercise:** Spend roughly 15–25 minutes with a familiar game. Select one short interaction and record what the player notices, attempts, receives as feedback, and tries next. Separate observation from interpretation.

Example observation: “I inspected three habitats before placing the creature.” Interpretation: “I may care about compatibility.” Alternative explanation: “The placement rules were unclear.” Avoid turning a single incident into a universal claim about players.

Replace “the combat is satisfying” with an account of timing, response, consequence, and perceived control.

**Milestone:** Describe a loop and two plausible explanations for one behavior. Distinguish the mechanic, observed response, and intended experience.

## 3. Change one rule

**Exercise:** Take a simple game or small existing mechanic and change one rule. Before playing, predict which behavior might change and why. Try the variant, compare with the original, and record an unexpected result.

For example, make movement consume a shared resource. Does that create interesting planning, unnecessary delay, or an unwanted experience?

Use paper, tokens, or a tiny digital model as appropriate. Physical prototypes are useful for decisions and resource relationships; use an interactive prototype when timing, motion, or feedback is the uncertainty.

**Milestone:** Explain a rule's indirect effects and costs. Preserve the original prediction alongside the result so the learning remains visible.

## 4. Build the smallest useful prototype

**Exercise:** Write an experience goal and one uncertain question. Build what is needed to investigate it within a suitable short timebox.

Example: “Will redirecting currents make guiding a ray feel playful?” Test current direction, movement, feedback, and recovery. A creature collection, shop, progression tree, and finished world are unnecessary for that question.

Avoid stripping away a component essential to the hypothesis. If character attachment is the question, recognizable behavior and presentation may matter more than an abstract token. If timing is the question, a paper description is insufficient.

**Milestone:** Produce a playable interaction and explain its evidential limits. A useful prototype may justify changing direction.

## 5. Observe another player

**Exercise:** Invite a willing person reasonably aligned with the intended audience. Give enough context to begin, then observe. Ask permission before recording. Do not coach unless the session would otherwise stop; record every intervention.

Note confusion, experiments, avoidance, and volunteered reactions. Ask neutral questions about expectations and outcomes; avoid seeking approval.

A single session can reveal a concrete issue; it cannot establish broad demand. Recruit fresh players for first-time comprehension questions. Returning players are useful for observing mastery, revised systems, and continued interest.

**Milestone:** Separate observations from opinions and identify an unanswered question. Label self-review explicitly when external testers are unavailable.

## 6. Revise against evidence

**Exercise:** Select one problem, generate two plausible explanations, and propose the smallest change that helps distinguish them. Predict what should differ in the next observation. Keep enough of the prototype stable to interpret the result.

If players ignore an ability, test whether they notice it before assuming it is weak. If they notice and understand it but still avoid it, inspect its usefulness or cost. Do not solve every uncertainty by adding rewards or tutorial text.

**Milestone:** Compare behavior before and after the change against the experience goal. State uncertainty. Judge the change on evidence and priorities, not implementation effort.

## 7. Finish a small experience

**Exercise:** Choose a project finishable within the available capacity. Define its beginning, playable middle, and meaningful ending or session boundary. List essentials and cut optional systems.

Include enough onboarding, feedback, recovery, and presentation for someone unfamiliar with the project to use it independently. Check relevant access needs and the intended delivery environment. Finish through an actual playable build, not only a design document.

**Milestone:** Another person can start, engage with the intended activity, and reach a meaningful boundary without the designer operating the game. Record remaining limitations and one production lesson before expanding scope.

## 8. Develop and assess a larger candidate

**Exercise:** Compare a few small concepts against the intended audience, distinctive appeal, production cost, and observed play. Choose a candidate based on a defensible tradeoff, then deepen a representative slice of it.

Test enjoyment, comprehension, and interest separately. A player understanding the hook does not prove sustained enjoyment; a favorable playtest does not establish commercial demand. Investigate audience interest with suitable external evidence when commercial success is the goal.

**Milestone:** Explain why this concept deserves the next investment, which assumption is most dangerous, and what evidence would justify continuing, reducing scope, or changing direction. Treat this as readiness for further development, not a promise of a breakout.

## 9. Coach toward independence

Give feedback in this order: observed strength, material gap, next experiment. Use plain language and examples from the learner's work. Offer reading for the current problem rather than assigning the entire bibliography.

Use AI to help implement, compare alternatives, organize notes, and inspect consistency. Ask the learner to make and explain the consequential design choices. Treat AI judgments and simulated players as hypotheses; retain real player observation as a separate source of evidence.

Reduce scaffolding as competence becomes visible. The desired outcome is a designer who can frame uncertainty, build an appropriate test, interpret imperfect evidence, and finish useful work independently.
